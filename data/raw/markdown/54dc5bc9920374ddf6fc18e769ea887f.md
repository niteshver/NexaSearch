# A Density-Matrix Framework for Electronic-Structure Analysis of Functional-Group and Salt Effects in Lithium-Metal Electrolytes
Mingkang Liu1, Huize Yu1, Yanbin Gao2, Nan Yao2,5, Xiang Chen2,3,4,* and Lei Shen1,6,7,* Department of Mechanical Engineering, National University of Singapore, 9 Engineering Drive 1, Singapore 117575, Singapore 2Beijing Key Laboratory of Complex Solid State Batteries & Tsinghua Center for Green Chemical Engineering Electrification, Department of Chemical Engineering, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China 3Institute for Carbon Neutrality, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China 4AI Solid-State Battery Innovation Center, Yibin, Sichuan, China 521C Innovation Laboratory (21C LAB), Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Limited (CATL), Ningde 352106, Fujian, China 6National University of Singapore Guangzhou Research Translation and Innovation Institute, Guangzhou, Guangdong 510700, China 7National University of Singapore (Chongqing) Research Institute, Chongqing 401120, China Corresponding authors. ###### Abstract
The reactivity of lithium-metal electrolytes arises from the interplay of molecular functional groups, Li+ solvation, and salt-anion participation. This interplay operates through the redistribution of electron density across donor, anion, and cation centers, which is most directly read out from the electronic structure resolved in space. Quantum-chemical calculations deliver such readouts faithfully, yet become computationally demanding across this multidimensional design space. Machine-learning electronic-structure models promise the needed throughput, but chemically diverse solvation shells remain underrepresented and predictions seldom extend to electrolyte-relevant readouts. Here, we present a density-matrix-centered AI platform (EMolStudio) for electronic-structure prediction and analysis. Its workflow integrates molecular functionalization, explicit Li+ first-shell assembly, density-matrix prediction with idempotency projection, and the electronic-structure analysis of frontier orbitals, electrostatic potential, Li+–donor bond order, and electron-localization descriptors. We apply EMolStudio to 163,655 functionalized molecules and 22,500 explicit Li+ first-shell clusters across four lithium salts. We find that 1) at the molecular scale, functionalization distinguishes CO2Me, CN, F/CF3, and sulfonyl groups by chemically distinct changes in frontier levels, electrostatic potential, and Li+–donor contact, consistent with \pi^{\ast}-acceptor, inductive, and polarization contributions, with sublinear accumulation at higher degrees of functionalization; 2) in explicit solvation shells, anion identity reshapes frontier-orbital localization: LiTDI anchors the HOMO on the anion across the entire library, whereas LiDFOB pairs an anion-hosted HOMO with strongly functional-group-dependent LUMO hosting. By carrying a unified density-matrix representation from molecular functionalization to salt-resolved solvation shells, EMolStudio translates functional-group and salt choices into electronic-structure hypotheses relevant to lithium-bond formation, desolvation, and interphase reactions.
Keywords: artificial intelligence-assisted electronic structure; lithium-metal electrolytes; functional-group effects; Li+ solvation chemistry; density-matrix descriptors; first-shell chemistry
## Introduction
Rechargeable lithium-metal batteries (LMBs) are widely recognized as a pinnacle technology for next-generation energy storage, owing to the ultrahigh theoretical capacity and low electrochemical potential of metallic lithium. However, their practical realization places stringent demands on liquid electrolyte chemistry, which simultaneously governs ion transport, metal deposition, desolvation, and the initial reactions that establish the solid-electrolyte and cathode-electrolyte interphases (SEI/CEI). As targets for energy density, fast charging, and operating voltage continue to rise, molecular stability, ion association, local electric fields, and interfacial side reactions become increasingly coupled design variables. Electrolyte design for LMBs should therefore establish a predictive connection between molecular architecture and the local Li+ solvation microenvironment where these crucial interfacial processes originate.
Molecular functionalization provides a versatile route to deconvolute and modulate these coupled variables. Fundamentally, introducing functional groups alters local electronic structures—such as spatial electron density distributions and orbital energy levels—which in turn dictates critical molecular properties, including redox stability, electrostatic potential (ESP) characteristics, and Li+ coordination affinity. This electronic-to-molecular property mapping establishes a powerful handle for rational electrolyte design. For example, rationally fluorinated ethers such as FDMB and F5DEE trace their cycling performance to the number and placement of fluorine atoms relative to the coordinating oxygens, surface-ESP extrema sort solvents into solvating and antisolvent regimes, and a normalized ion–solvent affinity places solvation structure, transport, and interphase behavior on a single design axis. The solvation environment requires equal attention because solvation, desolvation, and interfacial electron transfer collectively involve the Li+ first shell.
Once a functionalized molecule enters a practical electrolyte, its intrinsic electronic tendency participates in—and is constrained by—the surrounding coordination environment. For example, lithium-bond and lithium-ionic-bond interactions formed between Li+ and donor centers display different electron-localization and electrostatic signatures in Li-centered clusters, and these microscopic electronic perturbations are closely connected to desolvation and interfacial stability. Within a microenvironment (jointly constructed by solvent donors, salt anions, additives, and diluents), local coordination geometry and ion-association state further govern polarization, desolvation barriers, and the partitioning of redox-active frontier orbitals between solvent and anion fragments. High-concentration and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) demonstrate that controlling first-shell coordination chemistry and anion participation can redirect electrolyte reactivity and product composition.
To screen these coupled properties across large molecular and multicomponent solvation spaces, computational property prediction and force-field molecular dynamics have expanded the accessible candidate, formulation, and liquid-structure spaces. An early virtual-screening study ranked 7,381 carbonate derivatives by scalar reduction descriptors, and the Electrolyte Genome project automated first-principles ionization potentials, electron affinities, and redox windows across thousands of electrolyte molecules. Interpretable regression over elemental compositions identified low solvent oxygen content as a major correlate of lithium-metal cyclability, while a graph-enumerated library of 1,399 ether and carbonyl solvents—adopted here as parent scaffolds—showed that Li+ coordination lowers the solvent LUMO in most cases. Integrated platforms have further broadened this scope: the Uni-Electrolyte platform couples property prediction, generative design, retrosynthesis planning, and interphase reaction-network analysis over candidate pools exceeding molecules, while unified predictive–generative frameworks extend AI-assisted design to complete electrolyte formulations. In parallel, classical, ab initio, and machine-learning molecular-dynamics simulations resolve electrolyte solvation structure and transport, and machine-learned force fields now predict density, viscosity, and ionic conductivity for multicomponent formulations. Together, these advances support exploration of molecular, formulation, and solvation spaces at large scales. Spatially resolved electronic analysis of the resulting candidates and solvation structures is commonly performed with density functional theory (DFT).
Combined MD/DFT and cluster quantum-chemistry studies have delivered detailed pictures of how Li+ coordination and electrolyte additives reshape solvation motifs, frontier levels, reduction pathways, and interphase-forming tendencies. These approaches are well suited to testing targeted chemical hypotheses. At library scale, however, defining or extracting each Li+-centered cluster and evaluating it with an electronic-structure calculation can become computationally demanding across varied functionalization patterns, salts, solvents, and coordination topologies. Recently, machine-learning electronic-structure models have provided a complementary route to scaling such analyses. Equivariant models of Hamiltonians and related operators have been applied to molecular and crystalline systems, illustrating the broader development of learned electronic-structure methods. Chemically diverse electrolyte first shells, however, remain underrepresented, and reported evaluations typically stop at matrix or orbital-level accuracy. This gap persists because extending machine-learning electronic-structure prediction to these multicomponent environments requires both accurate matrix inference and a consistent route from the predicted electronic structure to electrolyte-relevant scalar and spatial readouts. For the downstream quantities considered here, the density matrix is well suited to this role because it is closely aligned with their physical definitions and provides a common basis for the ensuing analyses. An electrolyte-focused workflow can thus connect library-scale structures to ESP, dipole, bond order, ELF, and frontier-orbital localization within one analysis framework.
In this work, we present EMolStudio, an integrated platform that brings density-matrix analysis to electrolyte solvation chemistry at library scale. It combines molecular functionalization, explicit Li+-centered cluster generation, density-matrix prediction, electronic-structure parsing, and spatial visualization. Through these coordinated modules, the platform analyzes frontier orbitals, ESP surfaces, orbital localization, Li+–donor electronic contacts (bond order), and the electron localization function (ELF) for both isolated molecules and explicit first-shell clusters. Using a common density-matrix representation keeps these descriptors tied to the same electronic-structure basis. A domain copilot, EMolAgent, supports the interaction and orchestration of these multistep workflows.
After developing the density-matrix-centered integrated platform, we apply EMolStudio to two complementary electrolyte design spaces to validate it and to provide electronic-structure interpretation for molecular functionalization and solvation in lithium-metal electrolytes. The first is a library of 163,655 functionalized molecules that maps electronic responses across one to four functionalization steps and eight functional groups. The second is a library of 22,500 explicit Li+ first-shell clusters that examines how LiPF6, LiFSI, LiDFOB, and LiTDI, combined with candidate and background solvents, shape frontier-orbital localization, Li+–donor contacts, and Li-centered electronic-localization features. By establishing a direct pipeline from chemical structure to spatial electron distribution, EMolStudio translates functional-group and salt interactions into mechanistic hypotheses, offering a general framework for rational electrolyte discovery.
## Results and Discussion
### The EMolStudio Platform
EMolStudio is an electronic-structure analysis workflow for studying electrolyte chemistry at library scale, linking molecular design to solvation, desolvation, and interphase reactivity in lithium-metal electrolytes. The density matrix serves as its common representation for analyzing frontier orbitals (HOMO and LUMO), ESP, dipole moments, Li+–donor bond order, orbital-fragment localization, and Li+-centered ELF deformation descriptors.
EMolStudio combines input definition, structure generation, density-matrix prediction, electronic-structure postprocessing, and interactive organization. The input layer accepts user instructions, Cartesian coordinates, and simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) strings to define molecules, salts, and local solvation environments. EMolConf constructs functionalized molecules, assembles explicit Li+ first-shell clusters, and relaxes their geometries; EMolES learns the relation between relaxed structures and density matrices; EMolPipe converts density matrices into scalar properties and spatial electronic-structure objects; and EMolAgent, a language-model copilot, maps design questions to structured tasks and organizes interactive result views.

From user instructions, Cartesian coordinates, or SMILES strings, EMolConf builds functionalized molecules and explicit Li+ first-shell clusters and relaxes their geometries. EMolES predicts the density matrix of each relaxed structure as a shared representation for downstream analysis, EMolPipe parses it into scalar descriptors and spatial visualization objects, and EMolAgent maps user questions to design tasks and organizes the resulting analysis views. Outputs combine scalar properties with frontier-orbital isosurfaces, ESP-mapped surfaces, and Li+-centered ELF deformation descriptors.

#### EMolConf Structure Generation
EMolConf is the structure-generation layer of EMolStudio, converting functional-group edits and Li+ first-shell compositions into relaxed three-dimensional structures through a shared geometry-preparation and relaxation workflow based on the Universal Models for Atoms (UMA) potential, so that molecular and cluster analyses rest on a common structural basis.
To preserve the parent molecular configuration by construction, molecular functionalization is implemented as a rigid-body fragment attachment. Each functional group is stored as a three-dimensional template whose terminal marker atom defines its bonding vector, and attachment aligns this vector antiparallel to the parent C–H axis at a covalent-radius-derived bond length. A torsional scan about the nascent bond then selects a sterically clash-free rotamer, and valence checks with layer-wise International Chemical Identifier (InChI) deduplication keep the products unique and chemically valid through exact-depth enumeration of up to four functionalization steps per scaffold.
For Li+ first-shell cluster construction, EMolConf reads solvents, lithium-salt anions, and local compositions from the Agent-Translated Design Space produced by EMolAgent and generates initial local structures following the assembly sequence in Figure . Lewis-basic site mapping first identifies coordination ends that may face Li+, including carbonyl, ether, and sulfonyl oxygen, nitrile nitrogen, and fluorine. Initial coordination directions are then placed quasi-uniformly around Li+ using a Fibonacci sphere, and shell distances are adjusted according to fragment volume to reduce initial overlap when several fragments enter the first shell. Fragment orientations are further refined by constrained rotational sampling so that donor ends maintain reasonable Li+ contact directions. For sterically congested combinations, adaptive shell expansion increases the shell radius and repeats geometry checks until a relaxable local cluster candidate is obtained. Each assembled cluster additionally carries a dielectric constant, assigned by similarity matching to a curated 72-solvent library with composition weighting or fixed to a user-specified value, which sets the implicit-solvation environment of all downstream cluster calculations (Supporting Information, Section S1.2). The same scaffold–functional-group combination or the same Li+ first-shell composition can generate multiple initial structures to cover different local orientations and metastable coordination arrangements. After UMA relaxation, structures with the same chemical composition are ranked by relative energy, and the relaxed structures are then passed to EMolES density-matrix inference and EMolPipe electronic-structure parsing.

The Agent-Translated Design Space specifies the salt environment, solvent composition, and anion-excluded, single-anion-contact, or multianion-contact motif; the examples use ethylene carbonate (EC), 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), and bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI-). Using this workflow, we constructed an in-house electrolyte electronic-structure dataset for model development. The molecular subset starts from 1,399 electrolyte-solvent parent molecules and applies eight functional groups–methyl (Me), cyano (CN), trifluoromethyl (CF3), fluoro (F), methoxy (OMe), methoxycarbonyl (CO2Me), methylsulfonyl (SO2Me), and fluorosulfonyl (SO2F)–yielding 10,887 molecular structures after functionalization and Li+ coordination-geometry generation. The cluster subset contains 7,200 Li+ first-shell structures covering 72 common solvents and 10 lithium salts, spanning anion-excluded, single-anion-contact, and multianion-contact coordination. Representative outputs of molecular functionalization and cluster assembly are shown in Figures S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information; data generation, filtering, relaxation, and quantum-chemical settings are detailed in Section S1.
#### EMolES Density-Matrix Model
After relaxed molecular and/or cluster structures are obtained, EMolES uses the density matrix as the central electronic-structure representation of the EMolStudio platform. The density matrix provides a common theoretical basis for frontier orbitals, spatial ESP, dipole moments, local charges, and the Li+-centered ELF deformation factor, allowing these downstream descriptors to be derived consistently.
For density-matrix prediction, EMolES is built on the deep-learning tight-binding (DeePTB) framework and its local equivariant representations of quantum operators, uses SO(2) tensor-product operations inspired by the Equivariant Spherical Channel Network (eSCN), and introduces an electrostatic-aware message-passing (EAMP) module. EAMP applies keep-in-frame SO(2) tensor-product processing: after edge representations are transformed into a bond-aligned local coordinate frame, they remain in the same local reference frame during subsequent message passing and output preprocessing. Keeping edge features in this frame reduces repeated rotations and preserves edge-level electronic-structure information in high-order orbital channels. Because the density matrix is naturally blocked by atom pairs and orbital pairs, EMolES moves high-order equivariant self-interaction from the node level to atom-pair and orbital-pair edges, following the higher-body-order motivation of MACE. We refer to this edge-wise tensor-product self-mixing module as EACE, or edge-wise atomic cluster expansion. Before node aggregation, it represents directional charge rearrangement in local coordination environments such as Li+–donor and anion–solvent contacts. To balance expressivity and computational cost, this module uses channel-wise tensor products with a uvu connection format and OpenEquivariance acceleration. At the output end, the element-conditioned tensor-product layer modulates orbital-channel coupling according to atomic element and bond-type scalars, followed by fully connected tensor-product channel mixing before output. This design accommodates element-dependent differences in local charge distributions across multielement chemical environments.

Larger predicted electron-number error (PENE) is associated with larger downstream HOMO/LUMO energy, ESP, and dipole errors (Supporting Information, Figure S3). Accordingly, we introduce an idempotency-projected variant, EMolES-i, as a constrained postprocessing step for closed-shell systems, where “i” denotes idempotent. #### EMolPipe Electronic-Structure Readouts
EMolPipe is the electronic-structure postprocessing and property-parsing layer of EMolStudio. It links relaxed geometries, predicted density matrices, and spatial grids, converting model outputs into quantitative electronic-structure readouts with provenance; its electronic-structure and wavefunction-analysis routines build on PySCF and Multiwfn.
The resulting outputs fall into two categories. Scalar descriptors describe the local chemical environment, including HOMO/LUMO energies, gap, dipole moment, ESP extrema, Li+–donor bond order, Li+-centered ELF deformation factor, and fragment-resolved orbital weights. Spatial electronic-structure objects include HOMO/LUMO isosurfaces, ESP-mapped surfaces, and localized ELF distributions, which are used to analyze frontier-orbital distribution across coordination fragments and to inspect electronic polarization around Li+.
For large molecular and cluster libraries, EMolPipe exports files compatible with the Visualization for Electronic and Structural Analysis (VESTA) and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) programs and parallelizes descriptor extraction through the Portable Batch System (PBS) or DPDispatcher. Each output retains provenance links among the structure, density matrix, wavefunction file, cube file, and parsed descriptor, enabling aggregation by parent scaffold, functional-group type, lithium-salt environment, and first-shell topology. EMolPipe thereby organizes density-matrix predictions for chemical interpretation.
#### EMolAgent: Agent-Translated Design Space and Knowledge Copilot
EMolAgent is the interaction and physicochemical interpretation layer of EMolStudio. It translates a researcher’s natural-language question into a structured Agent-Translated Design Space by identifying candidate solvents, salt environments, target first-shell topologies, and requested electronic-structure readouts such as HOMO/LUMO, ESP, Li+–donor bond order, orbital localization, and Li+-centered ELF deformation. These structured definitions are dispatched to the integrated EMolConf–EMolES–EMolPipe workflow.
During execution, EMolAgent organizes automated molecular modeling, calls EMolES-i for density-matrix prediction and idempotency projection, and triggers electronic-structure postprocessing. The resulting physicochemical data are integrated into interactive views containing topology renderings, ESP surfaces, and energy-level comparisons, allowing researchers to evaluate key descriptors and adjust the design space.
EMolAgent also integrates a retrieval-augmented knowledge module linked to curated electrolyte-chemistry and computational-methodology resources. This module supports system-specific physical interpretation, parameter provenance, and follow-up analysis suggestions.
### Applications to Electrolyte Design Spaces
#### Electronic Effects of Molecular Functionalization
Using this workflow, we examine how molecular functionalization changes the electronic properties of an isolated electrolyte solvent before explicit solvation is introduced. This molecular view allows functional-group responses to be considered apart from the salt- and shell-dependent effects examined next. For each functionalized molecule, changes in the HOMO and LUMO energies, ESP extrema, dipole moment, and average Li+–donor bond order (BO) are reported relative to the corresponding parent scaffold under a consistent generation, relaxation, and analysis protocol (Supporting Information, Section S2).
Figure summarizes these functionalization responses relative to the corresponding parent scaffolds. The single-site set contains 19,457 matched structures covering the Me, OMe, F, CF3, CO2Me, CN, SO2Me, and SO2F groups, and the multisite analysis follows one to four functionalized sites across 161,081 matched structures (Supporting Information, Section S1.1). Throughout this analysis, HOMO and LUMO energies in molecular level serve as redox-relevant frontier-orbital descriptors rather than as direct measures of the electrochemical window, which is further shaped by explicit coordination, reaction free energies, interfacial potential, and degradation pathways. Likewise, ESP marks an electron-rich Lewis-basic pocket and ESP an electron-poor region of enhanced positive potential; together with solvating power, Li+ affinity, and local shell composition, these quantities constrain, but do not by themselves determine, solvation tendency.

The eight groups show distinct response patterns after single-site functionalization (Figure a,b; medians in Table S6). Me serves as an internal hydrocarbon reference, with near-zero median shifts in the frontier levels and the Li+–donor BO, and OMe provides a mild oxygenated reference that mainly adjusts the local oxygen environment and dipole moment. With Me as the reference, three broad patterns are apparent: stabilization of the low-lying unoccupied levels by CO2Me, CN, and the sulfonyl groups; HOMO stabilization by SO2F, CN, CF3, and F; and coupled changes in ESP, dipole moment, and Li+–donor contact for SO2Me and SO2F.
CO2Me and CN exemplify two distinct electron-accepting motifs. CO2Me shows a response consistent with carbonyl \pi^{\ast} acceptor character and the largest LUMO stabilization upon single-site functionalization among the eight groups (median LUMO =-0.983 eV), while its Li+–donor contact changes more moderately. CN combines the inductive withdrawal of the linear CN unit with its large bond dipole, lowering both frontier levels (median HOMO/LUMO =−0.406/−0.636=-0.406/-0.636 eV), reinforcing the local positive field (ESP{}_{\max}=+0.302 eV), and raising the dipole moment by D. These molecular responses provide a reference for interpreting LUMO localization in explicit first shells, where Li+ coordination can reorganize solvent frontier orbitals and reduction-relevant state localization.
CF3 and F display the inductive stabilization and local-field tuning characteristic of fluorination. Both functional groups lower the HOMO appreciably and raise ESP and ESP. CF3 shows larger local-field and Li+–donor-contact changes than a single F, and the scaffold-matched response planes of the Me/F/CF3 series show this graded fluorination trend (Supporting Information, Figure S7). These trends are consistent with the balance among fluorination degree, donor strength, and local coordination ability documented for fluorinated solvents and antisolvent/LHCE formulations.
The sulfonyl groups show the largest combined changes in local electric field, dipole moment, and Li+–donor contact. SO2Me reorganizes the local potential around its polar sulfonyl unit, giving the largest median ESP in the single-site set (+0.821 eV) together with the largest attenuation of the Li+–donor BO (-0.033). SO2F responds comparably in ESP (+0.808 eV) but adds deeper HOMO stabilization and the largest single-site dipole increase ( D). The lower BO is consistent with perturbation of the native Li+–donor environment alongside the increased polarity. This response is consistent with reported links among polar sulfone media, inner-shell structure, and interphase chemistry in sulfone-based and localized high-concentration electrolytes.
The strength of these responses also depends on the electrostatic background of the parent scaffold (Figure c; Table S7). SO2Me, SO2F, CN, and CF3 amplify the local positive field most effectively on parents whose initial ESP is low, and the increment converges once the scaffold is already electron-poor; ESP shifts analogously with the initial depth of the parent Lewis-basic pocket. Such scaffold–substituent coupling is consistent with classical Hammett-type substituent relationships and absolutely localized molecular orbital (ALMO) analyses, in which inductive effects, resonance, permanent electrostatics, and fragment-orbital mixing jointly set the frontier levels. A comparable interplay of scaffold and substituent has been reported in high-throughput surveys of organic optoelectronic molecules, where the two jointly delimit the accessible property window.
Following each group from one to four functionalized sites places these single-site effects on continuous trajectories (Figure d; endpoint values in Table S8, with the full depth-resolved distributions behind these medians in Figures S5 and S6). Me remains essentially flat across functionalization degrees, consistent with its use as the hydrocarbon reference. CO2Me deepens its LUMO stabilization from -0.983 to -1.537 eV while retaining a \pi^{\ast}-dominated response and more modest changes in Li+–donor contact. CN shows one of the largest cumulative LUMO stabilizations in the set (-2.482 eV at four functionalized sites) alongside progressively deeper HOMO stabilization, and CF3 and F build up inductive stabilization with increasing fluorination. SO2Me shows increasingly coordinated changes in the ESP extrema, dipole moment, and Li+–donor contact, with its median BO response growing from -0.033 to -0.129. For most groups, the single-site response direction is retained at higher degrees of functionalization, while the increments attenuate. Single-site statistics summarize the response associated with each functional group, and multisite trajectories show how these responses accumulate, often sublinearly, within a scaffold.
#### Salt-Resolved Li+ First-Shell Chemistry
Molecular functionalization tunes the electronic properties of a candidate solvent, while its reactivity in an electrolyte also depends on the local environment formed around Li+ by solvent donors, salt anions, and diluents. Explicit first-shell clusters connect these levels: on the same relaxed structure, they allow us to follow which fragment hosts the frontier orbitals, how strongly Li+ maintains electronic contact with its donors, and how electronic environment around Li+ changes. Lithium-bond and lithium-ionic-bond pictures distinguish the electron-sharing and predominantly electrostatic components of Li+ coordination. High-concentration and localized high-concentration electrolytes have highlighted anion entry into the first shell as an important factor in reaction pathways and interphase chemistry, and solvent–anion charge-transfer studies show that fragment coupling can influence orbital energies and localization in multicomponent shells.
The cluster library comprises 22,500 explicit Li+-centered first-shell structures spanning LiPF6, LiFSI, LiDFOB, and LiTDI, with 5,625 structures per salt and 7,500 per coordination motif (Supporting Information, Section S1.2). The library includes three fixed motifs: solvent-rich M2X (two candidate-solvent molecules and one anion), diluent-containing MBX (one candidate solvent, one background solvent, and one anion), and anion-rich MX2 (one candidate solvent and two anions). For every structure, the projected density matrix provides the HOMO, LUMO, and gap, the ESP, the total Li+–donor bond order, the Li+-centered ELF deformation factor, and fragment-resolved frontier-orbital assignments (Section S2; full statistics in Tables S10–S14).

Frontier-orbital hosting differs markedly among the four salts (Figure a; salt-pooled fractions in Table S10). LiPF6 clusters are dominated by solvent-derived frontier orbitals, with solvent-hosted fractions of 94.1% for the HOMO and 99.9% for the LUMO. LiTDI shows a contrasting occupied-state pattern: every one of its 5,625 HOMOs is anion-hosted, whereas 91.6% of its LUMOs remain solvent-hosted. LiFSI sits close to the HOMO partition boundary, with 46.6% solvent-hosted and 53.4% anion-hosted structures, alongside an almost completely solvent-hosted LUMO (99.9%), and LiDFOB pairs a predominantly anion-hosted HOMO (71.9%) with a predominantly solvent-hosted LUMO (71.1%). Together, these distributions capture solvent-derived frontier states, anion-derived occupied states, and mixed regimes across the four salts. These fractions describe the vertical frontier-orbital composition of the intact closed-shell cluster and do not by themselves set the reduction sequence. Anion-derived reduction is prominent in many concentrated and localized high-concentration electrolytes; for FSI- in particular, the favorable reduction reflects the relaxed reduced state and the dissociative reaction free energy of the anion rather than a low-lying vertical LUMO of the neutral shell. The hosting statistics therefore indicate where reduction-relevant states reside, while their ordering requires reduced-state and reaction-pathway calculations.
The LiTDI pattern is consistent with recent work on self-compartmented LiTDI electrolytes: Ou et al. showed that TDI- enriches at the cathode interface, promotes coordinated anion-rich solvation structures, and raises the HOMO energy of the multi-anion motif Li+–4FSI-–1TDI- relative to free 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) and other key solvation structures. Within the first-shell library studied here, the TDI anion hosts the HOMO throughout, while most LUMOs are solvent-hosted and vary with functionalization.
Within the salts of mixed character, frontier-orbital localization also varies with molecular functionalization. For LiFSI, the solvent-hosted HOMO fraction ranges from 20.9% for CN to 71.3% for OMe, while the LUMO remains almost entirely solvent-hosted for all eight functional groups. For LiDFOB, LUMO hosting is strongly group-dependent: 97.9% of CN structures are solvent-hosted, compared with 0.4% for OMe and 0.9% for Me. LiTDI keeps an anion-hosted HOMO for every group, with Me and OMe LUMOs close to the solvent/anion boundary and the remaining classes predominantly solvent-hosted. Salt identity largely sets the dominant localization regime, while functionalization further modulates LUMO hosting within it. These localization patterns provide an electronic-structure perspective on the reported roles of DFOB-- and TDI--derived species in film formation, interphase construction, and anion-derived passivation.
Total Li+–donor bond order condenses the aggregate electronic contact of the first shell into a single quantity (Figure b; Tables S10 and S11). The salt-pooled medians rank LiFSI (0.863) and LiDFOB (0.858) above LiPF6 (0.811), with LiTDI lower (0.698). Coordination topology has its largest effect for LiDFOB, whose median rises from 0.852 in M2X and 0.848 in MBX to 0.948 in anion-rich MX2. LiFSI reaches its highest median in the diluent-containing MBX motif (0.888), whereas LiTDI changes little across motifs (0.696–0.701). In this library, aggregate Li+–donor contact therefore varies primarily with salt identity and first-shell topology, with LiDFOB showing the largest response to anion-rich coordination. This ordering is consistent with the donor capacity of the anions themselves: the chelating oxalato oxygens of DFOB- add Li+ contacts as further anions enter the shell, whereas the charge-delocalized TDI- anion sustains the weakest aggregate contact.
The Li+-centered ELF deformation factor varies over a narrower range: the salt-pooled medians span 1.0306–1.0323, and within each salt shows no systematic relation to the total bond order (|r|\leq 0.19). Bond order and ELF deformation thus probe complementary aspects of first-shell chemistry: the integrated strength of Li+–environment electronic contact and the reorganization of electron localization around Li+. Functional-group effects on the total bond order are smaller than the salt and topology contrasts: SO2F gives the lowest pooled median (0.806), and the combined sulfonyl subset (median 0.814) sits below the remaining groups (0.832) (Table S13). Across this library, frontier-orbital placement varies more clearly with molecular functionalization, whereas Li+-centered contact is more strongly associated with salt identity and local topology.
## Conclusions
This work presents EMolStudio, a density-matrix-centered workflow integrating molecular functionalization, explicit Li+ first-shell construction, model inference, and chemical interpretation at library scale. EMolConf generates relaxed molecules and salt-containing coordination clusters through a common structural protocol; EMolES combines bond-aligned SO(2) message passing with EACE edge-wise self-mixing for atom-pair density-matrix prediction; idempotency projection restores the closed-shell electron-number and occupation constraints; and EMolPipe converts the resulting matrix into frontier orbitals, ESP, dipole, bond-order, orbital-localization, and ELF readouts organized by EMolAgent. Among the unprojected models, EMolES gives the lowest density-matrix errors on the in-house molecule and cluster tests, and projection further improves the density matrix and derived descriptors. The workflow is also evaluated on a first-shell cluster containing a lithium-salt environment absent from training and along a 1000-frame Li+ ligand-exchange path; against 30 DFT validation frames, the binding-energy mean absolute error is 0.084 eV.
Analysis of 163,655 functionalized molecules characterizes the electronic responses associated with eight functional groups. CO2Me provides the largest single-site LUMO stabilization (-0.983 eV), consistent with a carbonyl \pi^{\ast} acceptor response, whereas CN lowers both frontier levels while strengthening the local positive field. F and CF3 show a graded fluorination trend in frontier-level and electrostatic tuning, and SO2Me/SO2F produce the largest combined changes in polarity, ESP, and Li+–donor contact. Across one to four functionalized sites, the single-site response direction is generally retained, with diminishing increments at higher degrees of functionalization. The library thus shows how functional groups differ in acceptor-level tuning and broader donor and electrostatic changes.
The 22,500 explicit first-shell clusters show how salt identity and coordination topology modulate these molecular trends. LiPF6 retains predominantly solvent-hosted frontier orbitals, whereas all 5,625 LiTDI HOMOs are anion-hosted. LiFSI lies near the HOMO partition boundary, and LiDFOB displays the largest functional-group dependence of LUMO hosting, spanning solvent-hosted fractions from 97.9% for CN to 0.4% for OMe. Salt identity largely shapes the dominant orbital-localization regime, while functionalization further modulates frontier-orbital hosting within mixed regimes. In parallel, total Li+–donor bond order responds mainly to salt identity and first-shell topology, whereas the narrower Li+-centered ELF deformation reports a distinct reorganization of local electron localization.
Together, these results suggest a hierarchical view: functional groups tune acceptor levels, local fields, and donor character; salt anions influence frontier-state localization in the first shell; and coordination topology modulates aggregate Li+–environment contact. These findings fundamentally expand the theoretical guidance available for rational electrolyte design and establish a solid foundation for future closed-loop experimental engines for autonomous electrolyte discovery.
