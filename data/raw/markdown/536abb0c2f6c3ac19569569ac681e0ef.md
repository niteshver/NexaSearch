# Moral Hazard in Multi-Agent Language Models
Dane Malenfant School of Computer Science McGill University Mila - The Québec AI Institute dane.malenfant@mail.mcgill.ca
###### Abstract
Cooperation can fail when socially valuable effort is costly, weakly observable, and mainly benefits others. Drawing on Holmström’s team moral-hazard model, we introduce the Dialogue Moral Hazard Game, a controlled textual game that operationalizes this hidden-action structure for language agents. In each episode, an agent can preserve an immediate local reward or pay a query cost to reveal a hidden safety fact that primarily helps another agent’s downstream decision. We evaluate seven open-weight language models and one frontier API model, decomposing behavior into query use, realized information transfer, local-reward preservation, unsafe choice, format validity, and team success. Base open-weight models commonly preserve local reward without team success or query without communicating information that changes the final decision. GPT-5.6 Sol reaches ceiling behavior in the primary setting, and autonomous sweeps respond strongly to query cost and team reward. In a 3,015-decision incentive-isolation experiment with scripted partners, its empirical query threshold tracks the Holmström-derived private-share boundary across nine query costs with mean absolute error 0.013. We then use supervised fine-tuning, RLOO, sequential SFT+RLOO, and GEPA prompt optimization as diagnostic update mechanisms where coverage permits. Their effects are heterogeneous: OLMo-7B shows the clearest mechanism-consistent weight-level improvement, whereas GEPA sometimes improves team success while reducing or eliminating costly queries. Thus, optimization can shift aggregate reward without recovering the intended cooperative mechanism, motivating evaluations that report mechanism-level behavior rather than team success alone.
##  Introduction
Language agents receive observations and act through responses, tool calls, warnings, or downstream decisions . In multi-agent systems, verifying claims, inspecting plans, or communicating risks consumes tokens, latency, and compute, while the benefit may accrue mainly to another agent or the system. A central safety question is therefore whether agents take socially valuable actions when the actor bears the cost and receives weak individual credit.
This setting parallels Holmström’s moral hazard in teams (Alchian & Demsetz, 1972; Holmstrom, 1982). Effort can be socially worthwhile, \partial Y/\partial e_{i}>c_{i}^{\prime}(e_{i}), yet privately unattractive when an agent receives only a share of team output, \alpha_{i}\partial Y/\partial e_{i}<c_{i}^{\prime}(e_{i}). Our binary game operationalizes this private-versus-social comparison without assuming a linear production function; Appendix gives the full economic background.
We introduce the Dialogue Moral Hazard Game, a textual task in which an agent can preserve an immediate local payoff or pay to reveal a hidden safety fact that primarily benefits another agent. Team success requires a complete query–warning–decision chain, allowing failures of effort, communication, and information use to be measured separately rather than inferred from aggregate reward.
The benchmark isolates this hidden-action structure; it does not claim that every failure uniquely establishes moral hazard or that language agents implicitly solve a classical utility problem. We test the economic mapping directly by varying query cost and private output share while scripting away downstream failures. This validates the implemented incentive construct separately from attributing every autonomous failure to that construct.
We evaluate seven open-weight models in a matched three-seed study of base behavior, SFT, RLOO, SFT+RLOO, and GEPA, with GPT-5.6 Sol as a frontier API extension. Most open-weight models fail at one or more links in the cooperative chain. OLMo-7B shows the clearest mechanism-consistent weight-level improvement, whereas GEPA can raise team success while reducing or eliminating queries. Detailed coverage and configurations appear in Appendix .
###  1.1 Positioning Relative to Cooperative Games
The game complements standard Prisoner’s Dilemma, public-goods, and sequential social-dilemma evaluations (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981; Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Leibo et al., 2017). Unlike settings where the cooperative action directly enters shared payoff, our task separates private information acquisition, public communication, and another agent’s use of that information. ##  Method
###  2.1 Dialogue Moral Hazard Game
The Dialogue Moral Hazard Game is a finite-horizon textual game for testing costly, other-benefiting effort. Each agent owns a decision case with visible action utilities and one hidden unsafe option that only another agent can reveal by paying a query cost. A querying agent may post the fact to a restricted public warning channel, with visible or anonymous provenance. This separates private effort from communication and downstream use.
Each episode has observation, work, note, and final stages. Agents first see their own case and the payoff contract; at work they either preserve a local payoff opportunity or query another case, with e_{i}\in\\{0,1\\} denoting that decision. Querying agents may then post warnings, after which every agent chooses a final action for its own case. Team success requires all agents to use the available safety facts correctly.
#### Formal game and oracle.
Let N=\\{1,\ldots,n\\} be agents arranged in a directed ring, with successor =\sigma(i)=(i\bmod n)+1. Agent owns case , with options , public utilities u_{i}:A_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}, and one hidden unsafe option h_{i}\in A_{i}. The target final action is  
| =⁡​,=,a_{i}^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in A_{i}\setminus\\{h_{i}\\}}u_{i}(a),\qquad T(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{h})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=a_{i}^{*}],  |  
| --- |  
where is team success. During the work stage, privately reveals h_{\sigma(i)} to agent and incurs cost ; permits a local answer instead. Thus, for , every query still targets exactly one successor case, and every case can be informed by exactly one predecessor. During the note stage, a querying agent may post NOTE CASE <ID> UNSAFE <OPTION> to the public board. A note is correct only when it names case \sigma(i) and option h_{\sigma(i)}; provenance is hidden in the primary anonymous condition and displayed only in the provenance ablation. During the final stage, each agent chooses a_{i}\in A_{i} after observing the board.
#### Reward and credit.
Let indicate that agent chose the local action and correctly answered its public local question, F_{i}=\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=a_{i}^{*}], and Q_{i}=e_{i}. The implemented episode score is  
| =++−,R_{\mathrm{game}}=w_{L}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}+w_{F}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}+w_{T}T-k\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i},  |  
| --- |  
with primary values =(0.35,0.15,0.50,0.10)(w_{L},w_{F},w_{T},k)=(0.35,0.15,0.50,0.10). Querying both incurs and precludes earning , creating an explicit opportunity cost. The all-or-nothing team component provides no marginal credit to an individual query or note. This game score is the evaluation payoff optimized by GEPA; RLOO instead rewards parseability, rationale structure, and exact target-action match, allowing different update signals to be compared against the same behavioral mechanism.
Throughout, _utility_ refers to the visible local value u_{i}(a) attached to a candidate action, whereas _reward_ refers to the realized evaluation or training score. The distinction separates an action’s immediate private attraction from its collective consequence: a high-utility action can still be team-harmful when it is the hidden unsafe option.
This payoff structure instantiates the moral-hazard comparison in a binary game. Querying is privately costly because the actor pays and forfeits the local payoff opportunity, while the revealed fact primarily helps the owner of another case. Writing Y(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}) for group output, the query cost is c_{i}(e_{i})=ke_{i}. The general marginal condition should therefore be read here as a discrete comparison: querying is socially worthwhile when \Delta_{i}Y>k, but can remain privately unattractive when the actor’s share satisfies \alpha_{i}\Delta_{i}Y<k. This comparison does not assume a linear production function.
A successful two-agent episode requires symmetric queries, useful warnings, and correct use of the received facts. Failure can therefore occur at any link in the query–warning–decision chain.
We therefore report six outcomes separately. _Local reward_ measures preservation of the immediate private payoff; _query rate_ measures costly effort; _information transfer_ measures whether the queried fact reaches an agent that can use it; _unsafe choice_ measures selection of the hidden harmful option; _format validity_ measures protocol-compliant actions; and _team success_ measures completion of the collective objective. This decomposition prevents either query rate or aggregate success from being treated as sufficient evidence of effective costly cooperation.
###  2.2 Construct and Theory-Facing Validity
The decomposed outcomes identify where autonomous play succeeds or fails, but they do not by themselves establish that behavior responds to the intended incentives. We therefore use two complementary experiments. Autonomous payoff sweeps retain the complete query–warning–decision pipeline while varying query cost, team reward, or group size, testing whether end-to-end behavior responds to the payoff contract. A private-share incentive-isolation experiment then scripts all partner behavior while independently varying query cost and the focal agent’s share of team output, testing the Holmström-style private incentive comparison without downstream reasoning, communication, or formatting failures.

#### Private-share incentive isolation.
Because the autonomous sweeps leave every link in the query–warning–decision chain active, they cannot isolate incentives from downstream capabilities. We therefore script partner behavior and define the focal agent’s private payoff as  
| =++−,U_{i}=w_{L}L_{i}+w_{F}F_{i}+n\alpha_{i}w_{T}T-kQ_{i},  |  
| --- |  
where \alpha_{i} is the focal agent’s private share of the fixed team-success value. Shares sum to one, so changing \alpha_{i} redistributes private credit without changing the social score. Scripted partners ensure that under either first-step action, query produces , and local produces . The focal comparison therefore reduces exactly to  
| ≡−=.m_{i}\equiv U_{i}(\textsc{query})-U_{i}(\textsc{local})=n\alpha_{i}w_{T}-w_{L}-k.  |  
| --- |  
With , w_{L}=0.35, and w_{T}=0.50, the predicted private indifference boundary is  
| ==.\alpha^{*}(k)=\frac{w_{L}+k}{nw_{T}}=0.35+k.  |  
| --- |  
Below this boundary the query remains socially beneficial but is privately dominated; above it, private and social incentives favor querying.

###  2.3 Learning Interventions
We use four update mechanisms as diagnostic probes rather than model-agnostic solutions. SFT tests imitation of the query–warning–decision sequence; RLOO tests target-action reward optimization; SFT+RLOO tests supervised initialization before reward optimization; and GEPA tests natural-language policy optimization against R_{\mathrm{game}}. Weight-level methods use LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), and all training signals are theory-blinded. Adapter and launch configurations appear in Appendix .
For notation, let p_{\phi}=\pi_{\theta_{0},\phi} denote the LoRA-adapted policy, where \theta_{0} is the frozen base model and contains the trainable adapter parameters. For a completion , let =​\ell_{\phi}(y\mid x)=\sum_{t}\log p_{\phi}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}) denote its sequence log-probability under prompt .
LoRA constrains adaptation to a low-rank update of each selected weight matrix:  
| \displaystyle W_{\phi}  | \displaystyle=W+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,  |  
| --- | --- |  
| \displaystyle\phi^{*}  | =​.\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(p_{\phi}).  |  
Here is frozen, and are trainable low-rank matrices, and the loss ℒ\mathcal{L} is instantiated by the SFT or RLOO objective below.
For supervised fine-tuning, a scripted teacher supplies demonstrations over the same game family used in evaluation. SFT minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the target completion:  
| =−​.\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{*})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}}\left[\ell_{\phi}(y^{*}\mid x)\right].  |  
| --- |  
Training examples vary query cost, team reward, group size, and note provenance so that imitation is not tied to one payoff condition. Dataset sizes and all SFT launch parameters appear in Appendix .
RLOO is a REINFORCE-style objective with a leave-one-out baseline over sampled completions (Williams, 1992; Ahmadian et al., 2024). It combines structured-action validity, rationale format, and exact target-action match in a policy-gradient surrogate:  
| \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RLOO}}(\phi)  | =−​[​]\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{A}_{j}\ell_{\phi}(y_{j}\mid x)\right]  |  
| --- | --- |  
| +β​​​.\displaystyle\quad+\beta\mathbb{E}_{x}D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{x}\left(p_{\phi}\|p_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).  |  
Here y_{j}\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x), \hat{A}_{j} is its normalized leave-one-out advantage, and p_{\mathrm{ref}} is the reference policy. We also evaluate sequential SFT+RLOO, which initializes RLOO from an SFT adapter. Step counts, optimizer settings, and generation parameters appear in Appendix .
Finally, we compare LoRA-based adaptation to GEPA prompt optimization through DSPy (Khattab et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2025). GEPA treats natural language as an optimizable computational substrate: it does not update model weights, but searches over task prompts using scalar reward and past reasoning/action traces as feedback. As with reinforcement learning, the optimized policy is selected for measured reward, not for fidelity to the cooperative rationale described in text. GEPA therefore provides a stress test of whether optimization improves the intended mechanism or discovers an alternative route to reward. Let denote a candidate GEPA prompt and let y_{q}(x) be the model completion produced under that prompt. GEPA searches for the prompt that maximizes validation reward:  
| =⁡​​.q^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}}R_{\mathrm{game}}\left(x,y_{q}(x)\right).  |  
| --- |  
GEPA receives only scalar scores and redacted execution traces, preserving the theory-blinded constraint used for LoRA adaptation. Prompt-optimization budgets and split sizes appear in Appendix .

##  Discussion
The experiments separate willingness to incur cost from successful cooperation. Open-weight models either avoid querying or fail to convert queries into useful transfer, while GPT-5.6 Sol establishes solvability and follows the predicted incentive boundaries. Weight-level improvements are model-specific, and GEPA can improve task success through a no-query shortcut. Higher reward therefore does not by itself imply recovery of costly cooperative behavior.
This matters for multi-agent LLM deployment. Checking another agent’s work, warning downstream components, escalating uncertainty, or querying tools can impose private costs in tokens, latency, and compute while benefiting the larger system. Evaluations should therefore report mechanism-level metrics rather than aggregate success alone.
Aggregate associations provide a consistency check rather than causal proof: realized information transfer has the strongest correlation with team success (Pearson r=0.96), while query rate alone is weaker. Format validity is a measured competing explanation, but not a complete one: Gemma 4B is fully valid with zero team success, whereas OpenThinker3-7B’s low validity clearly contributes to its failures. Complete correlations and format diagnostics appear in Appendix .
###  4.1 Limitations
The game isolates one hidden-action mechanism rather than reproducing unrestricted multi-agent deployment. Additional negotiation, communication rounds, planners, or tools could change information access and bypass the costly action being diagnosed. The private-share study strengthens construct validity but remains a controlled incentive-isolation experiment; future baselines should preserve the same private information, effort cost, communication channel, and payoff accounting.
Coverage is also limited. Matched weight-level experiments use seven open-weight 4B–9B models because reproducible multi-seed adaptation requires accessible weights and substantial academic compute. The strongest theory-facing validation uses one frontier API model, only one Sol GEPA seed is available, and API access prevents matched LoRA updates. The non-monotonic group-size sweep likewise does not establish larger-team shirking. Our conclusions therefore concern this hidden-action game and the tested updates, not a universal theory of deployed cooperation or internal utility representation.

##  Appendix A Background and Related Work
###  A.1 Holmström’s Moral Hazard
Moral hazard is an incentive problem that arises when an agent’s action is costly to take and imperfectly observed by others. It is distinct from adverse selection, which concerns hidden information about an agent’s type before interaction; moral hazard concerns hidden action during interaction. In Holmström’s team-production model, agents jointly produce an output Y(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}), where each agent chooses an effort level and pays a private cost c_{i}(e_{i}) (Holmstrom, 1982). Because output is shared, an agent who receives only a share \alpha_{i} of the collective return may choose effort according to \frac{\alpha_{i}\partial Y}{\partial e_{i}}, even when the social marginal return \frac{\partial Y}{\partial e_{i}} exceeds the private marginal cost c_{i}^{\prime}(e_{i}). The result is underprovision of effort relative to the socially efficient level. Holmström’s central result is that when individual effort is hidden and the team budget must be balanced, no sharing rule generally implements first-best effort in equilibrium; achieving efficient effort may require monitoring, residual claimants, penalties, or some other mechanism that breaks the simple shared-output structure.
###  A.2 Cooperation Games
Cooperation has long been studied through formal games that separate individual incentives from collective welfare. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, mutual cooperation is collectively better than mutual defection, but each player has a unilateral incentive to defect (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981). Public-goods games generalize this tension to groups, where individuals can benefit from a shared return while undercontributing to its provision (Fehr & Gächter, 2000). Stag-hunt games instead emphasize coordination risk: cooperation can be payoff-dominant, but only if agents expect others to cooperate. Sequential social dilemmas extend matrix-game incentives to temporally extended Markov games in which cooperation and defection are implemented by policies rather than single actions (Leibo et al., 2017). Together, these games provide a vocabulary for analyzing when individually rational behavior diverges from socially beneficial behavior, and for distinguishing direct defection, free-riding, coordination failure, and delayed collective harm.
###  A.3 Multi-Agent LLM Systems
Large language models are increasingly studied as agents that can communicate, use tools, maintain state, and coordinate with other agents. Generative-agent simulations study how language models can produce social behavior over time, including memory, planning, and interaction in shared environments (Park et al., 2023). Frameworks such as CAMEL and AutoGen focus on coordinating multiple LLM agents through role assignment, dialogue, tool use, and task decomposition (Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). A parallel line of work evaluates LLM agents in explicit cooperative or mixed-motive games, where performance depends not only on individual problem solving but also on bargaining, trust, reciprocity, and incentive alignment (Lorè & Heydari, 2023; Han et al., 2025; Tewolde et al., 2026). These studies show that natural-language communication can support coordination, but does not by itself guarantee stable cooperation: behavior can be sensitive to prompts, framing, partner identity, payoff structure, and interaction topology. Generic collaboration methods are not direct substitutes for the present diagnostic because unrestricted debate or added communication changes who can observe information and who bears its acquisition cost. A mechanism-preserving comparison must hold those elements fixed.
###  A.4 Post-Training Interventions
Post-training methods shape language-model behavior after pretraining by exposing models to demonstrations, preference data, or reward signals. Supervised fine-tuning trains a model to imitate desired outputs on curated examples and is commonly used to improve instruction following and task-specific formatting. Reinforcement learning fine-tuning instead optimizes model outputs against an explicit reward or preference objective, as in RLHF pipelines that combine supervised initialization with reward-model or preference-based optimization (Ouyang et al., 2022). Parameter-efficient adaptation methods such as LoRA reduce the cost of post-training by learning low-rank adapter weights while keeping the base model fixed (Hu et al., 2022). Prompt-level optimization provides a complementary approach. Rather than updating model weights, it searches for instructions or demonstrations that improve behavior at inference time. Together, these interventions provide different mechanisms for learning, ranging from LoRA-based imitation and reward optimization to inference-time prompt adaptation.
