# SpecPrefetch: Parameter-Efficient Expert Prefetching for Sparse MoE Foundation Models
Jinwei Kong1,2,* Runqi Meng2,* Wentao Qiu1,3 Yongjian Zhou4 Zhenhua Ge 1 StepOs 2 ShanghaiTech University 3 Xiamen University 4 Chongqing University kongjw2023@shanghaitech.edu.cn gezhenhua@ustc.edu * Equal contribution. Corresponding author 
###### Abstract
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models expand foundation model capacity through conditional expert activation, but their full expert pools remain difficult to deploy under limited accelerator memory. Although expert offloading alleviates memory pressure by moving inactive experts to host memory or storage, it introduces a routing-dependent transfer bottleneck: required experts are known only after native top- routing, which serializes routing, expert loading, and expert execution during inference. To address this bottleneck, we propose SpecPrefetch, a parameter-efficient prefetching framework for offloaded MoE inference. SpecPrefetch uses a shared lightweight adapter to predict next-layer expert candidates only for asynchronous transfer, while the frozen native router still determines the final executed experts. By separating transfer prediction from execution routing, SpecPrefetch reduces exposed expert-loading latency without changing pretrained routing semantics, so prediction errors affect transfer efficiency rather than model outputs. In addition, a window-aware scheduler prioritizes feasible transfers under cache and bandwidth constraints. Across Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, SpecPrefetch achieves the best average expert recall in 9 out of 10 model-benchmark settings with substantially fewer trainable parameters than learned predictor baselines. On a Snapdragon 8 Elite device, SpecPrefetch further improves decoding throughput by up to over a compute-optimized offloading runtime, demonstrating practical benefits for storage-constrained MoE deployment. ##  Introduction
Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) and vision language models (VLMs) has been driven by scaling model capacity, training data, and computation. However, dense scaling increasingly strains inference cost, memory capacity, and serving throughput. Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures provide an attractive alternative by expanding total parameter capacity while activating only a small subset of experts for each token. Modern MoE models organize expert computation through pure routed expert pools or shared plus routed expert designs (Qwen Team, 2025; Jiang et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2024). Despite architectural differences, both designs expose the same deployment bottleneck. Sparse activation reduces per token computation, but the full expert pool must still be stored or made accessible during inference. As foundation models move from cloud datacenters to commodity GPUs and edge platforms, this storage-computation gap becomes a critical bottleneck under limited memory, and local compute.
Expert offloading provides a natural response to the dense expert footprint of MoE models under memory constrained deployment. Instead of keeping the entire expert pool resident on the accelerator, offloading places inactive experts in CPU memory or host-side storage and transfers selected experts to the active device on demand. However, expert demand is not known statically. In each MoE layer, the top- experts are determined only after the preceding hidden states are computed and evaluated by the router. As a result, expert transfer cannot be issued until the routing decision becomes available, which serializes hidden state computation, routing, host-to-device transfer, and expert execution along the inference critical path. Thus, the central challenge is not only reducing transfer cost, but also revealing expert demand early enough to overlap transfer with ongoing computation.

As shown in Figure , existing approaches mitigate exposed expert-transfer latency from three perspectives, but their design choices introduce different limitations. Transfer-cost reduction methods, such as quantization and compression, reduce the communication volume of expert movement . However, they do not alter the timing at which expert demand becomes available: required experts are still identified only after native top- routing, so expert transfer remains serialized after routing and can still lie on the critical path before expert execution. Training-free prefetching methods, represented by FATE, exploit cross-layer routing regularity to anticipate expert demand without parameter updates . While lightweight and portable, their prediction accuracy depends on stable routing correlations, which may not consistently hold across model architectures, tasks, and modalities. Training-based prediction methods estimate expert activations using auxiliary predictors, parameter-efficient tuning, draft modules, or pre-gating mechanisms (Hwang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Leviathan et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Although these methods can improve expert recall, coupling prediction with routing or execution may perturb the original expert activation pattern and potentially affect model behavior. Therefore, an effective prefetching solution should predict next-layer expert demand early for transfer scheduling, remain parameter-efficient, and preserve native routing for final expert selection.
To address these limitations, we propose SpecPrefetch, a parameter-efficient prefetching framework for offloaded MoE inference. SpecPrefetch formulates prefetching as next-layer expert prediction: given the representation at layer , a shared lightweight adapter predicts a compact candidate set for layer . The candidates are used only for asynchronous host-to-device transfer, while the native router at layer still selects the final top- experts for execution. By decoupling transfer prediction from execution routing, SpecPrefetch improves expert availability without altering pretrained routing semantics. At runtime, a window-aware scheduler turns predicted candidates into feasible transfer decisions by considering prediction confidence, cache residency, and transfer completion time. We validate SpecPrefetch through both offline expert prediction experiments and real-device mobile evaluation. Across Qwen- and DeepSeek-style MoE architectures, SpecPrefetch improves next-layer expert coverage with low trainable overhead; on a mobile device, it further translates prediction gains into practical latency reduction under storage-constrained offloaded inference.
To summarize, we make the following contributions:
  * •
We identify _learned transfer-only prefetching_ as a distinct design point for offloaded MoE inference.
  * •
We develop SpecPrefetch, a lightweight adapter-based framework that predicts next-layer experts and schedules feasible asynchronous transfers under runtime constraints.
  * •
We validate SpecPrefetch through offline prediction experiments and real-device mobile evaluation, showing higher expert coverage, low trainable overhead, and practical latency reduction.


##  Related Work
###  2.1 Sparse MoE Foundation Models
Mixture of Experts (MoE) scales foundation models through conditional computation. In sparse MoE layers, a router activates only a small subset of experts for each token, increasing total parameter capacity while limiting activated computation (Shazeer et al., 2017). Early systems such as GShard, Switch Transformer, and GLaM show that sparse routing improves model capacity and language modeling performance with controlled computation cost, while introducing challenges in routing stability, load balancing, communication, and expert specialization (Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022; Du et al., 2022).
Recent MoE models further diversify expert architecture. Mixtral uses a pure routed expert pool (Jiang et al., 2024), DeepSeekMoE separates shared and routed experts , and sparse upcycling initializes MoE models from dense checkpoints to reduce training cost (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023). These designs differ in how expert capacity is constructed, shared, and routed, which directly affects offloading and expert prefetching.
###  2.2 Offloaded MoE Inference and Expert Prefetching
Expert offloading enables large sparse MoE models to run under limited accelerator memory by keeping inactive experts in CPU memory or host-side storage. Existing systems reduce GPU memory pressure through expert caching, placement, asynchronous transfer, and heterogeneous memory management (Shen et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025a; Fang et al., 2025a). One line of work reduces expert movement cost or frequency: cache-based methods retain frequently or recently used experts on the accelerator (Xue et al., 2024; Kamahori et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024), while quantization, compression, expert splitting, and fine-grained placement reduce transfer volume or improve cache flexibility (Yan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025b). These methods improve memory efficiency, but mainly optimize expert movement after demand is known.
Another line of work overlaps expert movement with computation through scheduling and prefetching. ExpertFlow, Klotski, LayerScope, and related systems use predictive scheduling, token rebatching, asynchronous I/O, pipeline execution, and expert-aware memory organization to improve MoE serving (He et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025a; Yu et al., 2025a; Abhimanyu Bambhaniya, 2024; Sun and Li, 2025). FATE further uses adjacent-layer gate information for training-free expert prefetching in edge inference . These methods show the value of prefetching, but training-free prediction depends on stable cross-layer routing regularity, and many scheduling systems focus on throughput-oriented serving with batching or token-flow reorganization.
Training-based methods estimate expert activations with learned routers, auxiliary predictors, pre-gating mechanisms, or parameter-efficient adaptation (Hwang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Gavhane et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Speculative generation methods create additional scheduling windows through draft-then-verify execution (Leviathan et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). In contrast, SpecPrefetch targets standard offloaded MoE execution: it predicts next-layer candidate experts only for transfer preparation, while the native router still selects the final top- experts. Thus, SpecPrefetch changes when experts are moved, not which experts are executed.
##  Method
SpecPrefetch is a parameter-efficient prefetching framework for offloaded MoE inference that anticipates the expert demand of layer from the router input available at layer . As illustrated in Figure , the predicted candidates are used only for asynchronous transfer preparation, while the native router still selects the final top- experts during execution, so SpecPrefetch hides exposed transfer latency without changing the routing semantics of the pretrained MoE model. Following this design, Section first formulates router-preserving next-layer expert prefetching as a transfer-only prediction problem; Section then introduces a lightweight next-layer gate calibration module to estimate expert priorities of layer from the representation available at layer ; and Section finally describes a window-aware runtime scheduler that converts predicted priorities into feasible asynchronous transfers under cache, queue, and bandwidth constraints.

###  3.1 Router Preserving Expert Prefetching
Consider an MoE model with MoE layers and routed experts per layer. Let \mathbf{X}^{j}_{b,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} denote the router input of token at layer . The native router selects the token-level top- expert set  
| =​,\mathcal{T}^{j}_{b,s}=\mathrm{Top}_{K}\left(R^{j}\left(\mathbf{X}^{j}_{b,s}\right)\right),  |  
| --- |  
and the batch-level expert demand is  
| \mathcal{T}^{j}=\bigcup_{b,s}\mathcal{T}^{j}_{b,s},  |  
| --- |  
where \mathcal{T}^{j} denotes the batch-level native expert demand at layer .
Given an anchor layer , SpecPrefetch predicts a candidate expert set for the next MoE layer:  
| ,,\mathcal{C}^{l+1}=P^{l}\left(\mathbf{X}^{l}\right),\quad\left|\mathcal{C}^{l+1}\right|\leq M,  |  
| --- |  
where is the next-layer expert predictor and is the prefetch budget. The candidate set \mathcal{C}^{l+1} is used only for early expert transfer. The executed experts are still determined by the native router at layer , which produces the actual demand \mathcal{T}^{l+1} from \mathbf{X}^{l+1}.
Let t_{l}^{\mathrm{pred}} denote the time when prediction is issued at layer , and let t_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}} denote the time when layer begins routed expert computation. The available prefetch window is  
| =−.W_{l}=t_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}}-t_{l}^{\mathrm{pred}}.  |  
| --- |  
A prediction is useful only when a required expert e\in\mathcal{T}^{l+1} is resident in accelerator memory before t_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}}. Since \mathcal{C}^{l+1} controls only expert placement, false positives may waste transfer or cache capacity, and false negatives may require on-demand loading. Neither case changes \mathcal{T}^{l+1}. Therefore, SpecPrefetch preserves native routing computation while changing only expert transfer timing.
###  3.2 Adapter-Based Expert Prediction
We implement the predictor with a single lightweight external adapter shared by the prediction branch. The pretrained MoE forward path is kept frozen, including all native routers and routed experts. Given the router input at layer , the adapter estimates the expert priorities of layer only for prefetch ranking. It does not calibrate, replace, or participate in the native router during execution.
Let \mathbf{X}^{l}_{b,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{D} denote the router input of token at layer . The shared adapter predicts next-layer expert logits through a low-rank mapping:  
| =,\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{l+1}_{b,s}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}^{l}_{b,s},  |  
| --- |  
where \mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times D} and \mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times r} are shared across all anchor layers. This introduces trainable parameters in total. The predicted next-layer expert probability is then computed as  
| =softmax​.\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{l+1}_{b,s}=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\hat{\mathbf{z}}^{l+1}_{b,s}\right).  |  
| --- |  
During training, the original MoE model follows its native forward path. The frozen router at layer provides the detached teacher distribution  
| =softmax​,\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{T}}^{l+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\mathbf{X}^{l+1}\mathbf{W}_{g}^{l+1\top}\right),  |  
| --- |  
where \mathbf{W}_{g}^{l+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times D} is the native gate weight of layer . The shared adapter is trained to approximate this next-layer routing distribution using only the representation available at layer :  
| =​KL​(∥).\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLP}}=\sum_{l}\frac{\sum_{b,s}m_{b,s}\,\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{T},b,s}^{l+1}\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{b,s}^{l+1}\right)}{\sum_{b,s}m_{b,s}}.  |  
| --- |  
At runtime, token-level probabilities are aggregated into batch-level expert priorities:  
| =​.s_{e}^{l+1}=\sum_{b,s}m_{b,s}\hat{P}_{b,s,e}^{l+1}.  |  
| --- |  
The candidate expert set used for prefetching is selected by  
| =​.\mathcal{C}^{l+1}=\mathrm{Top}_{M}\left(\\{s_{e}^{l+1}\\}_{e=1}^{E}\right).  |  
| --- |  
Thus, a shared adapter predicts next-layer expert candidates for prefetching, while frozen native routers determine the executed experts.
###  3.3 Window-Aware Runtime Scheduling
Finally, the window-aware scheduler turns predicted next-layer expert priorities into asynchronous host-to-device transfers. For each anchor layer , it receives the priority scores \\{s_{e}^{l+1}\\}_{e=1}^{E} and candidate set \mathcal{C}^{l+1}. The scheduler maintains the resident expert set \mathcal{G}(t), the pending transfer set \mathcal{U}(t), and the transfer queue \mathcal{Q}(t), with each expert indexed by the layer-expert pair (l+1,e).
At scheduling time , the scheduler estimates whether each candidate can arrive before the target layer begins routed expert computation. Let \hat{t}_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}} denote the estimated execution time of layer , and let \hat{c}_{e}^{l+1}(t) denote the estimated completion time for transferring expert . The feasibility indicator is  
| =𝕀​[≤].\phi_{e}^{l+1}(t)=\mathbb{I}\left[\hat{c}_{e}^{l+1}(t)\leq\hat{t}_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}}\right].  |  
| --- |  
The runtime priority combines prediction confidence, cache status, pending-transfer status, and transfer feasibility:  
| \displaystyle q_{e}^{l+1}(t)  | =​[]\displaystyle=s_{e}^{l+1}\cdot\mathbb{I}\\!\left[(l+1,e)\notin\mathcal{G}(t)\right]  |  
| --- | --- |  
| ⋅​[]⋅​.\displaystyle\quad\cdot\mathbb{I}\\!\left[(l+1,e)\notin\mathcal{U}(t)\right]\cdot\phi_{e}^{l+1}(t).  |  
The scheduler submits candidates with nonzero priority in descending q_{e}^{l+1}(t) order until the transfer queue is full. Submitted experts enter \mathcal{U}(t) and are moved to \mathcal{G}(t) after transfer completion.
When execution reaches layer , experts already in \mathcal{G}(t) are executed immediately; otherwise, they are loaded on demand. We measure prefetch effectiveness by ready recall:  
| ReadyRecall=,={:∈}.\begin{gathered}\mathrm{ReadyRecall}^{l+1}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{R}^{l+1}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{T}^{l+1}\right|},\\\ \mathcal{R}^{l+1}=\left\\{e\in\mathcal{T}^{l+1}:(l+1,e)\in\mathcal{G}\\!\left(t_{l+1}^{\mathrm{exec}}\right)\right\\}.\end{gathered}  |  
| --- |  
Ready recall measures the fraction of native-router-selected experts that are resident before routed expert computation begins. Since the scheduler only controls expert placement, unused prefetches or on-demand loads affect efficiency but not the pretrained MoE output.

##  Experiment
###  4.1 Experimental Setup
##### Models and Benchmarks.
We evaluate SpecPrefetch on two representative MoE foundation models, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, which cover different sparse expert architectures and routing behaviors. The benchmarks are organized into two workload groups. The VLM group includes ChartQA Test, OCRBench, and HallusionBench, covering text-rich visual understanding and multimodal hallucination evaluation. The LLM group includes GSM8K and HumanEval, covering mathematical reasoning and code generation. This setting allows us to evaluate next-layer expert prediction across model families and workload modalities.
##### Baselines.
We compare SpecPrefetch with three representative expert prediction baselines: FATE, Draft Model, and ProMoE. FATE represents training-free cross-layer prediction based on routing regularity, Draft Model uses an auxiliary predictor to estimate next-layer expert demand, and ProMoE represents a stronger learned predictor with a larger prediction module. For fair comparison, all predictors are evaluated under the same transfer-only prefetching protocol: predicted experts are used only for prefetching, while the native router determines the executed experts.
##### System Evaluation Settings.
We evaluate system-level performance through both simulation experiments and real-device mobile evaluation. The simulation experiments are conducted on Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B and DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny to quantify how next-layer expert prediction reduces exposed transfer latency and improves end-to-end inference speedup under controlled offloading settings. In these simulations, inactive experts reside in host-side memory or storage, and selected experts are transferred to accelerator memory according to native router demand. We further deploy DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny on a Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite device with an Adreno 825 GPU for real-device mobile evaluation, where the decoding pipeline runs on device and different storage-speed regimes are emulated by injecting controlled I/O latency into expert loading.
##### Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate each method from three perspectives: expert prediction quality, parameter overhead, and system efficiency. For expert prediction quality, we report next-layer expert coverage recall. Given the native batch level expert demand \mathcal{T}^{l+1} at the next MoE layer and the predicted candidate expert set \mathcal{C}^{l+1}, expert coverage recall is defined as  
| ExpertRecall=.\mathrm{ExpertRecall}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{T}^{l+1}\cap\mathcal{C}^{l+1}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{T}^{l+1}\right|}.  |  
| --- |  
##### Implementation.
We use AdamW with β=(0.9,0.999)\beta=(0.9,0.999), \epsilon=10^{-8}, learning rate , cosine scheduling, warmup ratio 0.03, gradient clipping of 0.5, bf16 precision, and train for one epoch. The maximum text length is set to 4096 for DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny and 2048 for Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B. All experiments are implemented with PyTorch, HuggingFace Trainer, DeepSpeed, and FlashAttention 2, and are conducted on 2\times 8 NVIDIA A800-SXM4-80GB GPUs.

###  4.3 Ablation and Parameter Efficiency
##### Ablation on predictor design.
We isolate the effect of adapter-based calibration by comparing SpecPrefetch with an MLP Predictor trained under the same next-layer expert supervision. While the MLP Predictor learns a generic mapping from current-layer representations to next-layer expert demand, SpecPrefetch calibrates the frozen target-layer gate with a lightweight adapter, making the comparison focus on the benefit of gate-aware prediction.
Tables and show consistent gains over the MLP Predictor. On Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B, SpecPrefetch improves average recall by +4.94\% on GSM8K and +11.62\% on HumanEval, and also improves all VLM workloads. On DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, the gains are larger, reaching +18.71\% on GSM8K and +28.33\% on HumanEval, with more than improvement across all VLM workloads. These results show that gate-aware adapter calibration provides more reliable next-layer expert coverage than a generic learned predictor.
##### Parameter overhead comparison.
To examine whether the recall gains come from larger predictors, Table compares the trainable parameter overhead of different predictor designs. On DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny, SpecPrefetch uses 1.63M trainable parameters, which is only 0.07M more than Single MLP, but far smaller than Draft Model and ProMoE with 26.40M and 32.69M parameters. This corresponds to only 6.2% and 5.0% of their trainable parameter counts, respectively.
The advantage is more evident on Qwen3-VL-MoE-30B. SpecPrefetch uses 12.95M trainable parameters, compared with 24.38M for Single MLP, 19.19M for Draft Model, and 414.45M for ProMoE. In particular, SpecPrefetch requires only 3.1% of the trainable parameters of ProMoE. These results indicate that SpecPrefetch improves next-layer expert prediction through parameter efficient adapter based calibration rather than predictor scaling, which is essential for prefetching because predictor overhead should not offset the latency saved by asynchronous transfer.

###  4.4 Real-Device Mobile Evaluation
We further evaluate whether next-layer expert prediction can translate into practical latency reduction on resource-constrained devices. The experiments are conducted with DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny on a Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite device with an Adreno 825 GPU. The model uses 4-bit groupwise quantized experts and contains 11 MoE layers, each with 64 routed experts and routed experts per token. Each quantized expert occupies 1.94 MB, resulting in 1.36 GB total expert storage. We compare three configurations: a load-on-demand baseline, a compute-optimized runtime that batches expert dispatch, and SpecPrefetch built on top of the compute-optimized runtime. For each configuration, we decode 30 tokens and report the average over five runs. Since DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny follows a shared-plus-routed expert design, its shared expert remains resident and provides a compute window that can partially overlap with routed expert loading.
Figure and Figure report throughput and speedup under different storage speeds. Under the fastest NVMe setting, SpecPrefetch brings limited additional gain, with the compute-optimized runtime and SpecPrefetch reaching 4.36 and 4.31 tokens per second, respectively. This is expected because hot-cache routed-expert reads take only about 0.15 ms, much shorter than the 2.0 ms resident shared-expert computation, so most I/O latency is already hidden by computation overlap. As storage becomes slower, routed-expert loading exceeds this overlap window and appears on the critical path. ##  Conclusion
We presented SpecPrefetch, a parameter-efficient prefetching framework for offloaded MoE inference. SpecPrefetch uses a shared adapter to predict next-layer expert demand only for asynchronous transfer, while the native router still determines final expert selection. This transfer-only design reduces exposed host-to-device or storage latency without changing pretrained routing semantics. Across pure routed and shared-plus-routed MoE models, SpecPrefetch improves expert recall over MLP, draft-model, and ProMoE-style predictors with substantially fewer trainable parameters. Real-device evaluation on Snapdragon 8 Elite further shows that these prediction gains translate into higher decoding throughput when routed expert loading lies on the critical path. These results highlight lightweight next-layer expert prediction as a practical mechanism for memory- and bandwidth-constrained MoE deployment.

##  Limitation
SpecPrefetch has several limitations. First, expert coverage recall is only a proxy for runtime benefit. A correctly predicted expert improves latency only when its transfer would otherwise be exposed on the critical path; therefore, the speedup depends on cache state, storage bandwidth, transfer-compute overlap, and runtime support for asynchronous loading. Under hot-cache or fast-storage settings, routed expert reads may already be hidden by computation, leaving limited latency for prefetching to remove. Second, although prediction errors do not affect model outputs because the native router still determines final expert selection, they can still reduce system efficiency: false positives may waste bandwidth and cache capacity, while false negatives still trigger on-demand loading. Third, SpecPrefetch uses a single shared adapter for parameter efficiency, which may not fully capture layer-specific routing patterns in MoE models with highly heterogeneous expert specialization. Finally, the current scheduler follows a local feasibility-aware strategy and does not jointly optimize global cache residency, eviction, cross-request reuse, or multi-user batching. Our real-device evaluation is also limited to DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny on a mobile platform, so broader validation on larger MoE models, different hardware backends, and server-side offloading environments remains necessary.

This section provides details of the datasets used in our experiments. We organize the datasets into two groups according to their roles: predictor training datasets and evaluation benchmarks. The predictor training datasets contain diverse visual-language data sources covering document understanding, OCR-centric reasoning, chart and figure interpretation, image captioning, visual instruction following, and compositional visual question answering. The evaluation benchmarks are held-out workloads used in the main experiments to assess next-layer expert prediction and inference efficiency.
####  A.1.1 Predictor Training Datasets
The predictor training data are grouped by task type to reflect the diversity of input structures and prompt formats.
##### Document and OCR-centric visual understanding.
This group includes DocVQA, InfographicVQA, OCR-VQA, ST-VQA, TextVQA, and TextCaps. These datasets focus on visual inputs with substantial textual content, including scanned documents, infographics, book covers, natural scene text, and text-rich images. DocVQA and InfographicVQA emphasize document and infographic understanding, where models must reason over visually structured text layouts. OCR-VQA, ST-VQA, and TextVQA focus on reading and reasoning over text embedded in images. TextCaps extends OCR-centric understanding to caption generation, requiring descriptions that incorporate visible text in the image.
##### Chart, figure, and structured visual reasoning.
This group includes FigureQA and VisText. FigureQA contains synthetic scientific figures and plots designed for visual reasoning over graphical attributes, trends, and relationships. VisText focuses on chart-to-text generation, where models produce natural language descriptions from chart images and associated structured information. These datasets provide structured visual inputs that differ from natural images and document-style layouts.
##### General visual-language alignment and instruction following.
This group includes GQA, LLaVA instruction data, LRV-Instruction, ShareGPT4V, ShareGPT-4o, InternVL-SA-1B-Caption, and the OpenImages-Caption subset. GQA emphasizes compositional visual question answering over objects, attributes, relations, and multi-step reasoning. LLaVA instruction data and LRV-Instruction provide multimodal instruction-following examples, with LRV-Instruction further including grounding-oriented positive and negative samples. ShareGPT4V and ShareGPT-4o provide model-generated detailed image descriptions and instruction-style annotations. InternVL-SA-1B-Caption and OpenImages-Caption contribute large-scale caption-style image-text examples covering broad natural image distributions.
####  A.1.2 Evaluation Benchmarks
The main evaluation uses held-out LLM and VLM workloads. For VLM evaluation, we use OCRBench, ChartQA-Test, and HallusionBench. OCRBench focuses on OCR-related multimodal understanding and text-rich visual reasoning. ChartQA-Test evaluates question answering over charts and structured visual information. HallusionBench evaluates multimodal reasoning in hallucination-sensitive scenarios. For LLM evaluation, we use GSM8K and HumanEval. GSM8K evaluates mathematical reasoning, while HumanEval evaluates code generation.
####  A.1.3 Dataset Summary
Overall, the dataset collection covers a broad range of input formats, including documents, infographics, scene-text images, charts, natural images, instruction-following samples, mathematical problems, and code-generation tasks. This diversity supports evaluation across heterogeneous LLM and VLM workloads without relying on a single task distribution.
