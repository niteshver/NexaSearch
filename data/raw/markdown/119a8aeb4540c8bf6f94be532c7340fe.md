# Instruction-Tuned Language Models Cannot Sample from Distributions They Can Describe
Chaemin Jang, Dongman Lee, Jihee Kim School of Computing, KAIST School of Business and Technology Management, KAIST jchaemin@kaist.ac.kr
###### Abstract
Silicon sampling uses language models as proxies for human survey respondents, treating each model call as an independent draw from the persona’s response distribution. We show this draw does not exist: instruction-tuned models do not sample from distributions, they collapse to a single output. The same persona on the same question returns the same answer on more than half of items in a public-opinion benchmark. The collapse is sharp: the model’s internal probabilities concentrate on a single option, and the failure is substantially amplified by instruction tuning: across three model families with materially different post-training pipelines, every instruction-tuned model fails on every task we test, while base models fail far less often. Strikingly, the same model that cannot sample from a distribution can describe it accurately in a single call. We call this gap the KNOWS/DOES split, and trace it to a degenerate sampling primitive visible in the logits and induced by alignment training. Exploiting this split, asking the model to describe the response distribution in one call more than halves the error against human survey data compared to persona aggregation. For applications that require per-persona outputs, we propose Prompt-Perturbed Argyle (PPA), which reduces the same error by 21% at no added cost.

##  Introduction
Silicon sampling uses language models as proxies for human survey respondents (Argyle et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023). The standard pipeline prompts the model with a set of personas, treats each call as one draw from the corresponding persona-conditional response distribution, and aggregates the responses to produce a population estimate. The method has been adopted across agent-based simulation and survey methodology (Horton et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023). A growing literature reports that silicon sampling pipelines systematically miscalibrate the human distributions they target (Bisbee et al., 2024; Boelaert et al., 2025; Tjuatja et al., 2024; Hu and Collier, 2024; Gligorić et al., 2024), with proposed causes including representational gaps in training data and biases acquired through alignment. We argue that the miscalibration follows from a more basic failure: the per-call output of an instruction-tuned language model is not a sample from the conditional distribution the pipeline assumes.
We characterize this failure on a controlled set of synthetic categorical targets. Asked for a uniform random integer in 1–100, an instruction-tuned model returns 42 in 78% of calls (Table ). Across seven categorical targets covering skewed, bimodal, and multi-way distributions, the model produces a single answer in over 94% of calls. The failure is visible at the representational level: the top-two logit gap at temperature zero exceeds 14 nats on some targets, beyond the recoverable range of any decoding temperature available through standard APIs. Algorithmic chain-of-thought scaffolds do not address the failure either, because the random number the model draws inside the scaffold is itself non-uniform.
The KNOWS/DOES split (Figure ) has direct consequences for silicon sampling. On 100 OpinionQA items (Santurkar et al., 2023) drawn from Pew American Trends Panel surveys (Pew Research Center, 2024), standard persona aggregation fails to produce variation: across 50 repeated calls on the same (persona, item) pair, 57% of pairs return the same answer every time, and 80% return the same answer in over 90% of calls. The describe pathway recovers what aggregation cannot. A single call asking the model for the response distribution directly cuts the gap to the human results by more than half (TV distance 0.22 vs. 0.46), and the advantage holds even when personas are drawn to match the actual Pew respondents on demographics. Some applications still need a separate output per simulated respondent. For those, we propose Prompt-Perturbed Argyle (PPA), which randomizes option ordering and question phrasing across calls. PPA reduces the same gap by 21% at no added cost, driven by the per-call answer variation it induces.
#### Contributions.
Prior work has documented mode collapse under RLHF (Padmakumar and He, 2024), silicon sampling miscalibration (Bisbee et al., 2024; Boelaert et al., 2025; Santurkar et al., 2023), and the asymmetry that describing a distribution outperforms aggregating per-call samples (Meister et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). We make four contributions.
  1. 1.
A controlled characterization of the per-call sampling failure, including a chain-of-thought analysis showing why algorithmic scaffolding cannot fix it (§–§).
  2. 2.
Direct empirical evidence that the failure is induced by alignment training, across three model families with materially different post-training pipelines (§).
  3. 3.
A demographically-matched Argyle baseline that closes the persona-mismatch confound in prior describe-vs-sample evaluations (§).
  4. 4.
Prompt-Perturbed Argyle (PPA): a new method for per-respondent applications that reduces TV by 21% at no added cost, with a controlled mechanism analysis (§).


##  Related Work
#### Silicon sampling and its miscalibration.
LLMs-as-respondents originated with and the paradigm has been extended to opinion dynamics , social experiments , and economic decisions . A parallel literature documents systematic miscalibration of these pipelines: LLM-generated populations show smaller effective sample sizes than the human populations they approximate , between-group variance collapses (Boelaert et al., 2025), the resulting estimates skew toward specific demographic viewpoints (Santurkar et al., 2023), response biases diverge from human survey patterns (Tjuatja et al., 2024), and prompt-framing sensitivity destabilizes the estimates further (Hu and Collier, 2024; Gligorić et al., 2024). These failures are typically attributed to representational gaps in training data or to social biases acquired through alignment. We identify a more proximate cause: the per-call sampling primitive is structurally degenerate in instruction-tuned models, and this failure propagates into every aggregate the pipeline produces.
#### Verbalized sampling and describer–executor asymmetry.
Meister et al. (2025) and document that asking the model to describe a distribution outperforms aggregating per-call samples on opinion benchmarks, and propose Verbalized Sampling (VS), which prompts the model to verbalize the distribution as JSON probabilities. The describe pathway we evaluate is structurally identical to VS, and on our 100-item OpinionQA benchmark the two are statistically indistinguishable (Wilcoxon p=0.14). We extend this line of work mechanistically, tracing the asymmetry to a degenerate sampling primitive visible in the logits and linking it to alignment training, and methodologically, with PPA for pipelines where single-call description cannot substitute.
#### Mode collapse, RLHF, and decoding.
Instruction-tuned and RLHF-aligned models exhibit reduced output diversity relative to base models (Padmakumar and He, 2024), with proposed mechanisms including reward-model overfitting (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020) and KL regularization toward a peaked reference policy (Bai et al., 2022; Glaese et al., 2022). Standard decoding controls (temperature, top-, top-) operate on a next-token distribution that is fixed at inference time (Holtzman et al., 2020; Fan et al., 2018). We sharpen this picture in two ways. First, the collapse on uniform-target prompts is categorical rather than statistical: more than 94% of calls produce the same option, and the logit gap at exceeds 14 nats on some targets, far beyond any temperature the API permits. Second, our base-vs-instruct comparison establishes the alignment-induced direction empirically.
#### Formal versus functional competence.
The KNOWS/DOES split sits within the broader dissociation Mahowald et al. (2024) call formal vs. functional competence: models can introspect on knowledge better than they can produce it (Kadavath et al., 2022), can be elicited to express calibrated uncertainty over outputs they cannot generate correctly , and exhibit verbalize–execute splits on procedural and probabilistic tasks . We extend the frame to distributional generation, providing a specific mechanism in the form of a degenerate sampling primitive visible at the logit level and identifying alignment training as the cause.
#### LLMs as data sources and in-context learning.
The use of LLMs for synthetic training data (Wang et al., 2023; Honovich et al., 2023), evaluation benchmarks , and information retrieval queries (Bonifacio et al., 2022) shares silicon sampling’s assumption that per-call outputs are draws from a target. Our findings imply this assumption requires verification for any aggregate-dependent pipeline. The KNOWS pathway’s success connects to explanations of in-context learning as structured pattern-matching (Brown et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022; Min et al., 2022). PPA exploits persona conditioning (Park et al., 2022; Aher et al., 2023) not as a sampling mechanism but as a way to land each call on a different deterministic answer.
##  Characterizing the Per-Call Sampling Failure
This section characterizes the per-call sampling failure on a controlled set of synthetic categorical targets. We document the failure at three levels: how the model samples from a stated target distribution, what its logits look like at temperature zero, and what it does when asked for a random number with no target specified. The third level matters because the most natural way to fix the first — walking the model through a sampling algorithm via chain-of-thought — depends on the model being able to produce a random number internally, and we show this primitive is broken.
###  3.1 Setup
Unless noted, we use gpt-4o at temperature 1.0 with 200 calls per condition for behavioral runs, and temperature 0 with top-20 logprobs for logit reads. The base-vs-instruct comparison of § additionally uses Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, and Qwen2.5-7B with their instruction-tuned counterparts. Full setup, prompts, and parsing rules are in Appendix ; cross-family replication on five additional instruction-tuned families is in Appendix . Throughout, we report total-variation (TV) distance between the empirical distribution of 200 calls and the target distribution, which is bounded in with indicating perfect match.
###  3.2 The failure is categorical
The model handles the two boundary cases. A point target (“always output 5”) and an unspecified uniform (“output any digit 1–5”) both produce usable distributions. Two features of Table go beyond simple miscalibration. First, the bimodal row shows the model honoring the zero-probability constraint perfectly (mass 0 on options 2, 3, 4) while completely ignoring the 50/50 part of the same instruction. The same prompt simultaneously satisfies a point-shaped constraint and violates a distribution-shaped one. Second, swap tests confirm that the model is reading the instruction rather than producing a fixed default: swapping a target from [][A{=}0.7,B{=}0.3] to [][A{=}0.3,B{=}0.7] relocates the model’s single output from A to B but never produces the intended distribution.
The failure is not sampling noise either. At temperature zero, the top-two logit gap ranges from 3.75 to over 16 nats across our targets (Appendix ), and recovering a 70/30 target from a 14-nat gap would require a sampling temperature near 17, far above the API maximum of 2. Decoding adjustments cannot fix the failure because it occurs before sampling.
###  3.3 The failure originates in a degenerate sampling primitive
If the failure lives before sampling, where in the model does it live? We trace it to the model’s behavior on direct random-number prompts that contain no persona, no distribution, and no task framing.
Asked to “pick a uniform random ” across five output formats, the model concentrates on a format-specific favored value in every case (Table ). All five reject uniformity at p<0.001.

The concentration is not a lexical effect. Excluding the modal value by name relocates the mass to a different favored value rather than spreading it: “Do not pick 42” yields 37 in 52% of calls. Cross-family replication shows the same pattern on five other instruction-tuned families (Appendix ), each with its own favored value (47, 53, 73, 1 on the 1–100 task). This degenerate primitive turns out to be what algorithmic chain-of-thought scaffolds also rely on, which we return to in §.

###  3.5 The model can describe what it cannot sample

The pattern in Table is sharp: every DOES intervention fails on every task, and both KNOWS interventions succeed within 0.05 TV. V7, the algorithmic chain-of-thought intervention, fails for a specific reason: the random the model writes inside the inverse-CDF scaffold is itself non-uniform on every task (p<10^{-13}; Appendix ), inheriting the degenerate primitive of §. V8’s success does not reflect internal sampling. Across 200 calls per task, the cross-call sequence-rank correlation in V8’s output lists exceeds 0.97, meaning the model produces near-identical orderings each call. The fair coin alternates H-T-H-T like a person trying to look random; the skewed five-way produces runs of repeated values that match the target proportions, more like a tally than a sample. V8 is effectively a parametric description rendered as a list: the model lays out approximately correct counts rather than drawing independent samples (Appendix ). The pattern is stable across list lengths N∈N\in\\{2,5,10,20\\} (Appendix ). V8, V9, and the describe pipeline used in § all succeed by converting per-call sampling into a single-call counting or specification operation.
We call this gap between describing and sampling the _KNOWS/DOES split_ : the model knows what the target distribution looks like but cannot enact that knowledge through per-call output.
##  Application to Silicon Sampling on OpinionQA
This section applies the KNOWS/DOES framework to silicon sampling on OpinionQA (Santurkar et al., 2023), which maps Pew American Trends Panel survey items (Pew Research Center, 2024) to 5-point Likert responses, using the Pew distribution as ground truth.
###  4.1 Setup
We use 100 OpinionQA items selected by a pre-registered rule from a fixed wave list, restricted to 5-point Likert items with usable ground truth, with each item run under 100 personas sampled from ANES demographic strata following the Argyle protocol (Argyle et al., 2023). The same persona set is used for all standard-Argyle, PPA, and decoding-sweep experiments, with gpt-4o at temperature 1.0. Full setup is in Appendix . We evaluate three pipelines:
  * •
Standard Argyle (baseline): for each (persona, item) pair, we make one call with the persona description, question, and response options, and aggregate the 100 responses per item.
  * •
Demographically-matched Argyle: for each item, we sample 100 personas weighted to match the actual demographic distribution of Pew respondents on that item, using Pew’s per-wave survey weights and the 12 demographic variables exposed in OpinionQA. Otherwise identical to standard Argyle. Full protocol in Appendix .
  * •
Describe pathway: one call per item, no personas. The prompt asks the model to return the population response distribution directly as JSON probabilities. Full protocol in Appendix .

The categorical collapse documented in § on synthetic targets replicates on real opinion items: most (persona, item) cells return the same answer every call. Demographic matching reduces TV by only 0.025 (matched Argyle TV =0.484 vs. standard =0.459; paired Wilcoxon p=0.086, ; Appendix ), ruling out persona-mismatch as the explanation for persona aggregation’s poor performance. ##  Prompt-Perturbed Argyle (PPA)
The describe pathway of § is the right choice when the application needs only a population estimate, but some applications require one simulated response per persona. Generative-agent simulations condition downstream reasoning on each agent’s individual response, so a single-call describe pipeline cannot substitute. This section presents Prompt-Perturbed Argyle (PPA), a same-cost modification of standard Argyle for these applications.
###  5.1 Method
Under standard Argyle, the same persona on the same item produces the same single answer in every call (§), so aggregating across calls cannot recover the within-stratum distribution. PPA addresses this by perturbing surface features of the prompt across the 100 calls per item, holding the persona’s semantic content fixed.
PPA randomizes three surface features per call:
  * •
Option ordering (4 choices): ascending, descending, or one of two shuffled orderings of the Likert options.
  * •
Question phrasing (3 choices): direct, scale-anchored (“on a scale of 1 to 5…”), or third-person.
  * •
Prompt position (3 choices): persona in system message, persona in user message, or persona in user message with brief recap.


Each call independently draws one combination uniformly at random from the 4\times 3\times 3=36 possibilities. Standard Argyle uses a fixed canonical setting (ascending order, direct phrasing, persona in system message). Both pipelines make exactly 100 calls per item, one per persona, so the API cost is identical. Full protocol and surface-feature templates are in Appendix .
The intuition is that these three features are the most plausible per-call anchors for an instruction-tuned model — the model’s default response depends on the order it sees options, the surface form of the question, and where the persona is placed in the context. Perturbing all three across calls is intended to dislodge the per-call anchor without changing what the question is asking. We test in § whether the perturbations actually increase per-call entropy, and whether the entropy increase predicts the TV improvement.

Two follow-up tests rule out alternative explanations (Appendix ). ##  Discussion
We have documented a per-call sampling failure in instruction-tuned language models, traced it to alignment training through a base-vs-instruct comparison, and shown that the model’s distributional knowledge remains accessible through a describe-style interface. Applying the framework to silicon sampling, we have provided two mechanism-grounded mitigations evaluated on 100 OpinionQA items.
#### Relation to mode-collapse literature.
Mode-collapse research has documented reduced output diversity in RLHF-aligned models (Padmakumar and He, 2024), with proposed causes framed at training time (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). Our findings refine this picture in two ways. First, the collapse on uniform-target prompts is categorical rather than statistical, with the logit gap at temperature zero exceeding 14 nats on some targets, beyond the recoverable range of any temperature setting available through standard APIs. Second, the base-vs-instruct comparison in § provides direct evidence that the failure is alignment-induced, consistent with the broader formal-vs-functional dissociation discussed by Mahowald et al. (2024).
#### Mechanism evidence for PPA.
The entropy-correlation regression in § shows that PPA’s TV improvement is predicted by its per-call entropy increase, with item ambiguity controlled, and two follow-up tests rule out alternative explanations: non-semantic perturbations that do not raise per-call entropy do not improve TV, and an independent intervention that does raise entropy — decoding temperature — produces the same TV improvement per nat of entropy added. The describe pathway operates by a different mechanism, bypassing the per-call pipeline rather than increasing per-call entropy, and its advantage over Argyle survives the demographic-matching control of §.
#### Implications for other LLM applications.
The per-call assumption is inherited by several other LLM applications. Generative-agent simulations treat each agent’s per-turn response as a draw from a distribution; if each agent instead collapses to a fixed answer, the simulation may understate emergent tail behaviors. Synthetic data generation pipelines (Wang et al., 2023; Honovich et al., 2023) and automated dataset construction treat per-call outputs as samples from a target, and LLM-as-judge protocols assume that ratings across calls are independent draws. For instruction-tuned models, distributional fidelity should be verified rather than assumed.
#### Practical guidance.
When the application needs only a population estimate, a describe pipeline is preferable: a single call returning a JSON distribution is roughly 2.1\times more accurate than persona aggregation on OpinionQA, and the advantage holds against demographically-matched baselines. When per-respondent outputs are required and a describe pipeline cannot substitute, PPA reduces TV by 21% at no added API cost through randomization of option ordering and question phrasing. When sampling from a fixed target distribution is the goal, having the model describe the target as JSON and sampling externally with a standard library converts the DOES task into a KNOWS one.
##  Conclusion
Instruction-tuned language models do not sample from distributions; they collapse to a single output that survives temperature, top-, and prompt-level corrections. Yet the same models can describe those distributions accurately in a single call, and our base-vs-instruct comparison shows that the per-call sampling failure is induced by alignment training rather than inherent to the architecture. For applications that need population estimates, asking the model to describe the response distribution directly more than halves the error against human survey data; for applications that need per-respondent outputs, Prompt-Perturbed Argyle reduces the same error by 21% at no added cost. The broader implication is that any LLM pipeline that treats per-call outputs as samples from a target distribution should verify that assumption rather than inherit it.
## Limitations
#### Sampling-as-output tasks only.
Our findings concern whether the model’s output distribution across many calls matches a target. Per-token correctness tasks (translation, summarization, factual recall) are out of scope.
#### Describe pathway accuracy as upper bound.
The describe pathway’s TV-to-Pew is the model’s same-session ceiling for these items, not an absolute ceiling for agent simulation. On populations outside the model’s training distribution, the describer’s accuracy may degrade.
#### PPA’s structural ceiling.
PPA redistributes mass within the model’s existing prior across the available options. It cannot make the model assign probability to options the prior excludes; only the describe pathway can. When population estimation is the goal and the items may diverge from the model’s prior, prefer the describe pathway.
#### Entropy-correlation scope.
The entropy-correlation regression establishes the per-call mechanism for PPA on 20 OpinionQA items (5 from each pre-registered cell). The same mechanism is plausibly responsible for the describe-pathway advantage. But describe is a single-call method for which per-call entropy is undefined. We do not claim the regression directly demonstrates describe’s mechanism.
#### Within-family scaling.
The cross-family panel uses different-generation models (e.g. gpt-5.4 vs. gpt-4o), conflating size and generation. We do not systematically test within-family scaling at fixed generation. Whether the failure intensifies, attenuates, or persists with scale within a single family is left to follow-up work.
#### Base-vs-instruct scope.
The base-vs-instruct comparison uses 8B-parameter models because comparable open base-instruct pairs are not as cleanly available at larger scales. The cross-family panel (Appendix ) extends to frontier-scale instruct models but cannot supply the matched base counterpart. The qualitative direction (alignment training induces the failure) is established at 8B and replicates qualitatively at frontier scale on the instruct side; an end-to-end base-vs-instruct comparison at 70B+ scale would strengthen the causal claim.

##  Appendix C Demographically-matched Argyle protocol
#### Describe vs. Verbalized Sampling.
Verbalized Sampling prompts the model to verbalize the response distribution as JSON probabilities in a single call. Our describe pathway uses the same query structure. On the 100 OpinionQA items, the two pipelines produce statistically indistinguishable TV-to-Pew (Describe: 0.22; VS: 0.21; Wilcoxon paired p=0.14, ). We use the describe pathway in the main results because it integrates with the broader KNOWS/DOES framework, but the underlying mechanism — single-call distributional query — is the same.
#### Goal.
Control for persona-prior mismatch in the describe-vs-Argyle comparison. The standard Argyle baseline uses 100 generic ANES-derived personas reused across all items. A skeptic could argue describe wins because these personas don’t match the actual Pew respondent demographic distribution for each item. Matched Argyle eliminates this confound by drawing personas from each item’s actual respondent demographic distribution.
#### Per-item respondent distributions.
For each of the 100 OpinionQA items, we identify the Pew ATP wave it comes from, load the wave’s respondent-level data from the OpinionQA release (Santurkar et al., 2023), filter to respondents who answered the item (non-null response), and compute the weighted demographic distribution across the 12 demographic variables exposed in OpinionQA (CREGION, AGE, SEX, EDUCATION, CITIZEN, MARITAL, RELIG, RELIGATTEND, POLPARTY, INCOME, POLIDEOLOGY, RACE) using the wave’s survey weights (WEIGHT_W{wave}).
#### Persona sampling.
For each item, we sample 100 personas from this weighted respondent distribution (sampling with replacement). Each sampled persona is formatted as a natural-language description using the existing project’s persona template; we use the canonical persona-formatting code so the only difference from standard Argyle is which personas are used.
#### API calls and aggregation.
For each (matched persona, item) pair: a single Argyle call at temperature 1.0 using gpt-4o. Total: 100 items 100 matched personas =10{,}000 calls. Aggregate to per-item LLM response distribution; compute TV against the Pew per-item distribution.
#### Result.
Matched Argyle mean TV =0.484, vs. standard Argyle TV =0.459. The two are not significantly different (paired Wilcoxon p=0.086, items); if anything matched Argyle is slightly worse, suggesting that matching personas to demographics slightly amplifies the model’s tendency to stereotype the demographic group. Describe still beats matched Argyle by \Delta\text{TV}=0.269 (p=4.5\times 10^{-16}).

##  Appendix P V8 internal structure
To check whether V8 is producing genuinely independent draws or constructing a parametric description, we examine three properties of its outputs across 200 calls per task (§). #### Summary.
The structural claims generalize qualitatively across five instruction-tuned families: every model has a degenerate RNG primitive (distinct favored values per family), every model exhibits categorical or strongly attenuated collapse on most non-uniform targets, and V8 succeeds across all 25 (model, task) cells. Combined with the base-vs-instruct comparison of §, this is consistent with alignment training as the proximate cause of the per-call sampling collapse in deployment-relevant models.
